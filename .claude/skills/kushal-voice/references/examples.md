# Before and after

Every pair below is a real rejection from the portfolio work, with what
replaced it. Read them when a draft feels fine but you are not sure it is.

## Openings

**Rejected**, "very childish"

> I build software people use at work every day. Lately a lot of that is AI.
> Eight years across healthcare, banking and automotive. Most of what I do
> sits between the database and the screen, and I've spent enough time either
> side of that to be useful there too.

Why: no facts. The first sentence is true of every enterprise developer.
"Lately a lot of that" hedges twice in five words. "Between the database and
the screen" is a coy way of not saying full-stack. "Enough time… to be useful"
is an apology.

**Accepted**

> Three industries in eight years. The code was never the hard part.
>
> Prescriptions at Express Scripts, a forecasting tool traders used at Bank of
> America, now dealership software at automotiveMastermind, part of S&P Global
> Mobility. Each time, the domain took longer to learn than the stack did.
>
> Lately that has meant LLM systems in a regulated industry. Same job as it
> always was: work out what people actually do all day, then build the thing
> that helps.

Why it works: the headline is a claim he has already made twice in his own
notes. Three employers named. AI arrives in the last paragraph as the current
chapter, not the identity.

## Credit

**Rejected**, "feel like I did everything… so offensive and feel arrogant"

> I own features from database to pixel.

**Accepted**

> I was one of the engineers on it, working alongside product and design.

And where he did lead, once, without decoration:

> First place at the company hackathon, out of a field of more than a hundred
> engineers. I led our team.

## Describing a project

**Rejected**, because it reduces the work to its least impressive reading

> The assistant that drafts a salesperson's follow-up.

**Accepted**

> An LLM assistant on the deal sheet, drafting the call script, email or text
> a salesperson would otherwise write from scratch. It began as an idea I
> pitched at the 2025 hackathon, where it made the top three. I worked across
> the .NET service behind it: streaming inference against Gemini, the
> compliance rules, and what gets stripped out before anything reaches the
> model.

## Lessons from a project

**Rejected**, "are you writing poem or what?"

Aphorisms with no mechanism behind them.

**Rejected**, "too much compliance, should be top level points… which anyone
can understand"

Implementation detail that only someone on the team could follow.

**Accepted**

> Wiring the model is the easy part. Agents and tools took days. Proving the
> output was good enough to send took the rest of the year.

> AI has to check AI. A second model scores every message against a written
> contract, and QA can change that contract without an engineer.

> A number a user can't trust is worse than no number. The count on the badge
> and the rows in the list have to come from the same query.

> Deleting a morning's work costs less than defending it for a month.

The shape: a claim anyone could use, then the concrete thing that earned it.
Two sentences, sometimes one.

## Scale

**Rejected**, "2 vs 12 team does not make sense"

> Beat eleven other teams.

**Accepted**

> Out of a field of more than a hundred engineers.

The denominator has to be the one a reader would assume.

## Register

Avoid, in order of how often it creeps back in:

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| an em-dash | a full stop, or a comma, or a colon |
| "leveraged", "utilised", "spearheaded" | used, built, led |
| "passionate about" | nothing; show it |
| "cutting-edge", "innovative" | the thing itself |
| "helped deliver" | what he did |
| "open to opportunities in X" | nothing; the work says it |
| "achieved a top-three placement" | "it came third" |

## One he wrote himself

When he corrected the Fritz timeline he wrote: *"most of Fritz work went last
year."* That is the register, flat, specific, no adjectives. Drafts that
sound like that sentence tend to survive review.
